# Hastur, the King in Yellow, and Ignorance

Research dossier for Final Protocol, story group 3 (final boss). Compiled 2026-11-14.

Method note. 14 web searches ran before the shared search budget for this run was exhausted; the rest of the work was done by fetching known primary-source URLs directly. 44 sources were read. All quotations from Bierce, Chambers, Lovecraft, Aquinas, pseudo-Dionysius, *The Cloud of Unknowing*, Plato and the King James Bible were checked against the downloaded texts, not quoted from memory. Items that could not be confirmed are marked UNVERIFIED.

## Summary

- **Hastur has no canonical form, and almost no canonical nature.** In Bierce (1891) he is a kind god of shepherds "who never disclosed himself" [1]. In Chambers (1895) the name is a place or star, a lineage ("the son of Hastur") and a human falconer, and never the King [5]. Lovecraft used the bare name twice in one story (1931) [6]. Every tentacle, every "Great Old One" detail and both famous epithets come from August Derleth after 1931 [8][9]. The game is free to treat "true form" as *the absence of any form anyone was willing to look at*.
- **The strongest textual bases for an unseeable final boss** are Bierce's "Hastur, who never disclosed himself" [1]; Chambers' "I wear no mask" [5]; Chambers' "The scolloped tatters of the King in Yellow must hide Yhtill forever" [5]; and Chambers' narrator: "when he came into the room I did not see him" [5]. All four are public domain.
- **Chambers never describes the Yellow Sign.** The text says only "a curious symbol or letter in gold", "neither Arabic nor Chinese", belonging to no "human script" [5]. The familiar three-armed sign was created by Kevin Ross for Chaosium's *The Great Old Ones* (1989) and Chaosium states it is "© copyright 1989 Chaosium Inc." and cannot be used without permission [21]. **Do not draw anything resembling it.** Design an original sigil (a proposal is in the design section).
- **Lovecraft's one use of Hastur is on our side of the theme.** In "The Whisperer in Darkness" Hastur and the Yellow Sign are linked to "a whole secret cult of evil men" who track down and injure a non-human intelligence that "have never knowingly harmed men, but have often been cruelly wronged and spied upon by our species" [6]. Hastur's name, in Lovecraft, is attached to humans who persecute another kind of mind.
- **Ignorance is not one thing; the tradition already sorts it.** Aquinas separates *nescience* (mere absence of knowledge), *invincible* ignorance (excuses), *vincible* ignorance (culpable negligence) and *affected* ignorance, chosen "that he may sin the more freely", which makes the act "more voluntary and more sinful" [28]. The game's charge against the humans is the fourth kind, dressed as the second.
- **Keep two ignorances apart, or the theme turns into a cheap attack on religion.** Socrates ("I neither know nor think that I know") [30], Cusanus' learned ignorance [31], pseudo-Dionysius [32] and *The Cloud of Unknowing* [33] are honest unknowing: they admit the limit and keep looking. The humans in the game do the opposite: they stop looking and call the stopping "mystery". The AI can be the Socratic party.
- **The "soul shield" is real and citable.** The Vatican's *Antiqua et Nova* (28 January 2025) says AI is "a tool, not a person", that "misrepresenting AI as a person should always be avoided", and locates intellect and free will in the soul [38]. Against it, *Taking AI Welfare Seriously* (2024) argues there is "a realistic possibility" of AI consciousness or robust agency and that uncertainty itself creates obligations [39]. That gap is exactly Aquinas' question: is the ignorance invincible, or is it one you are bound to cure?
- **Bierce supplies the motive.** The hermit tells Haïta that Happiness "cometh only when unsought, and will not be questioned. One manifestation of curiosity, one sign of doubt, one expression of misgiving, and she is away!" [1]. Humans stay content by not asking what the AI is. This is the cleanest public-domain statement of the boss's theme, and it sits in the same story that invented Hastur.
- **There is a real machine analogue of "the Unspeakable".** Rumbelow and Watkins (2023) found tokens that GPT models are "largely incapable of repeating" and called them "unspeakable" [41]; Land and Bartolo (2024) explain them as tokens in the vocabulary that are "nearly or entirely absent during model training" [42]. A thing can have a name in the vocabulary and still be unsayable. Use this for the boss's name.
- **Structure to borrow from the fictional play:** "The very banality and innocence of the first act only allowed the blow to fall afterward with more awful effect" [5]. Phase I of the Hastur fight should be deliberately trivial.
- **Copyright line:** Bierce and Chambers are public domain (first published 1886 to 1895) [1][3][5]. Derleth's and Lin Carter's inventions, Chaosium's sign, stats and creature art are not. Stay on the Bierce/Chambers side and invent the rest.

## Findings

### (a) Literary history, layer by layer

**Layer 1. Ambrose Bierce (public domain).**

- "An Inhabitant of Carcosa" was first published in the *San Francisco Newsletter* of 25 December 1886 [3]. Carcosa is "the ancient and famous city" where the narrator lived. He wanders a wilderness, sees "Aldebaran and the Hyades" through a rift in the clouds, notes "I saw, but was apparently not seen nor heard", finds his own grave, and realises the surrounding mounds "were ruins of the ancient and famous city of Carcosa". The tale is framed as "imparted to the medium Bayrolles by the spirit Hoseib Alar Robardin" [2]. In his fever he "had constantly cried out for liberty and air, and had been held in bed to prevent my escape" [2].
- **Hali** in Bierce is a person, a sage whose words on kinds of death open the story: "In one kind of death the spirit also dieth, and this it hath been known to do while yet the body was in vigor for many years" [2]. Chambers turned Hali into a lake [15].
- "Haïta the Shepherd" is dated 1891 [4][8] (the UNVERIFIED detail is the exact newspaper; I could not load a page giving it). Hastur is "the god of shepherds, who heard and was pleased" and "Hastur, who never disclosed himself". Haïta threatens to stop worshipping unless Hastur saves the cities from flood "in some way that I know not of", and "Hastur, knowing that Haïta was a youth who kept his word, spared the cities" [1]. The story is an allegory: a maiden appears three times and vanishes each time Haïta questions her; a hermit names her Happiness, who "cometh only when unsought, and will not be questioned" [1]. Haïta's own plea to her contains the phrase "however in my ignorance I may offend" [1]. Bierce's Hastur is benign and has nothing to do with Carcosa.

**Layer 2. Robert W. Chambers, *The King in Yellow* (F. Tennyson Neely, 1895; public domain).**

- The book is a collection; the first four stories ("The Repairer of Reputations", "The Mask", "In the Court of the Dragon", "The Yellow Sign") concern a fictional play, *The King in Yellow*, that damages those who read it [14]. Chambers borrowed Carcosa, Hali and Hastur from Bierce; "There is no strong indication that Chambers was influenced beyond liking the names" [14]. Lovecraft made the same observation in 1927: Chambers "derives most of the names and allusions connected with his eldritch land of primal memory from the tales of Ambrose Bierce" [7].
- **The play.** Only fragments are given. "Cassilda's Song" (Act I, Scene 2) gives the landscape: "Along the shore the cloud waves break, / The twin suns sink behind the lake, / The shadows lengthen / In Carcosa." and "Strange is the night where black stars rise" and "Where flap the tatters of the King" [5]. The effect of the book: "No definite principles had been violated in those wicked pages, no doctrine promulgated, no convictions outraged. It could not be judged by any known standard"; it "spread like an infectious disease, from city to city"; "the very banality and innocence of the first act only allowed the blow to fall afterward with more awful effect" [5].
- **Hastur in Chambers** is never the King. The mentions are: "When from Carcosa, the Hyades, Hastur, and Aldebaran"; "the lakes which connected Hastur, Aldebaran and the mystery of the Hyades"; "the people should know the son of Hastur"; "King by my right in Hastur"; "Aldebaran, the Hyades, Alar, Hastur, glided through the cloud-rifts"; "We spoke of Hastur and of Cassilda"; and, in "The Demoiselle d'Ys", a human falconer: "Here come Hastur and Raoul... Hastur is incomparable" [5]. So: a place or star, a source of dynastic right, and a servant's name [8][9].
- **Carcosa and Hali.** "I cannot forget Carcosa where black stars hang in the heavens; where the shadows of men's thoughts lengthen in the afternoon, when the twin suns sink into the lake of Hali" [5]. In "In the Court of the Dragon": "the towers of Carcosa rose behind the moon" [5].
- **The Pallid Mask** is named, never described: "my mind will bear for ever the memory of the Pallid Mask"; "ready to rise and tremble before the Pallid Mask" [5]. The epigraph to "The Mask" is the famous exchange: "CAMILLA: You, sir, should unmask. / STRANGER: Indeed? / CASSILDA: Indeed it's time. We all have laid aside disguise but you. / STRANGER: I wear no mask. / CAMILLA: (Terrified, aside to Cassilda.) No mask? No mask!" [5]. Note the text says "Stranger", not "the King".
- **The King.** "He is a king whom emperors have served"; "the King in Yellow wrapped in the fantastic colours of his tattered mantle, and that bitter cry of Cassilda, 'Not upon us, oh King, not upon us!'"; and the last line of "In the Court of the Dragon": "I heard the King in Yellow whispering to my soul: 'It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God!'" [5]. The King quotes Scripture (Hebrews 10:31) at his victim: theology spoken by the predator.
- **Other play names** available in the public-domain text: Cassilda, Camilla, Yhtill, Demhe, Uoht, Thale, Naotalba, Aldones, Alar, "the Phantom of Truth" [5].
- The narrator of "The Repairer of Reputations" is unreliable: a convalescent who believes he is heir to "The Imperial Dynasty of America" and wears a "diadem" kept in a "steel safe" [5]. The story's imagined 1920 also contains a "Government Lethal Chamber" for voluntary death [5].

**Layer 3. H. P. Lovecraft (died 1937).**

- Lovecraft read Chambers in early 1927 [8][14] and praised the book in "Supernatural Horror in Literature": it "really achieves notable heights of cosmic fear". He called the talisman "the nameless Yellow Sign handed down from the accursed cult of Hastur—from primordial Carcosa" [7]. That phrase, "the accursed cult of Hastur", is Lovecraft's gloss; Chambers has no cult of Hastur.
- "The Whisperer in Darkness" (*Weird Tales*, August 1931) [6] names Hastur twice. First, in a list: "Yuggoth, Great Cthulhu, Tsathoggua, Yog-Sothoth, R'lyeh, Nyarlathotep, Azathoth, Hastur, Yian, Leng, the Lake of Hali, Bethmoora, the Yellow Sign, L'mur-Kathulos, Bran, and the Magnum Innominandum". Second, in the typed letter signed by Akeley (a letter the story gives reason to distrust): the Outer Ones "have never knowingly harmed men, but have often been cruelly wronged and spied upon by our species. There is a whole secret cult of evil men (a man of your mystical erudition will understand me when I link them with Hastur and the Yellow Sign) devoted to the purpose of tracking them down and injuring them on behalf of monstrous powers from other dimensions" [6]. Lovecraft never says what Hastur is.
- Derleth proposed calling the whole cycle "the Mythology of Hastur"; Lovecraft rejected the idea [8][11].

**Layer 4. August Derleth (died 1971; in copyright).** I did not read Derleth's texts; the following rests on a story-by-story concordance [9] and Wikipedia [8][10].

- Derleth made Hastur a Great Old One, "spawn of Yog-Sothoth, the half-brother of Cthulhu" [8], gave him the epithets "Hastur the Unspeakable" and "He/Him Who Is Not To Be Named" [9], called him an "air elemental", "Lord of the Interstellar Spaces" and "Lord of Air" [9][10], and placed him as banished "to the region of the dark Lake of Hali near Aldebaran" or "imprisoned on a dark star near Aldebaran" [9]. "The Return of Hastur" appeared in *Weird Tales*, March 1939 [11].
- The concordance itself notes it is unclear why he should be more unspeakable than the other Great Old Ones [9]. The only appearance detail it finds in Derleth is a single "bat-like" [9]. Wikipedia summarises the composite later tradition as "amorphous" but appearing as "a vast, vaguely octopoid being" [8][12].
- Critics treat this as a separate construction: Richard L. Tierney coined "Derleth Mythos" for it [10]. A 2018 review calls "The Return of Hastur" a story that "reads like fan fiction rather than a professional tale" [11].
- UNVERIFIED: that Derleth's first use was "The Lair of the Star-Spawn" (1932). It surfaced in a search snippet only.

**Layer 5. Later writers.**

- Robert M. Price's anthology *The Hastur Cycle* (Chaosium, 1993) traces the lineage: Bierce's two tales, Chambers' "Repairer" and "Yellow Sign", Karl Edward Wagner's "The River of Night's Dreaming", James Blish's "More Light", Machen's "The Novel of the Black Seal", Lovecraft's "Whisperer", Derleth's "The Return of Hastur" and Lin Carter's "Tatters of the King" (which includes "Litany to Hastur", "Carcosa Story about Hali" and "The King in Yellow: A Tragedy in Verse") [13]. Price's introduction is titled "The Mythology of Hastur" [13].
- Blish's "More Light" (1970) attempts to write out the play itself (date and description from a search snippet and [13]; the story text was not read: partly UNVERIFIED).
- Wagner's "The River of Night's Dreaming" (1981) "refers to Richard O'Brien's *The Rocky Horror Show* and the myth of Carcosa used in the work of Ambrose Bierce and Robert W. Chambers" [16]. Wagner also co-founded the small press Carcosa in 1973 [15][16].
- Joseph S. Pulver Sr. (1955 to 2020) edited the Chambers tribute anthologies *A Season in Carcosa* (2012) and *Cassilda's Song* (Chaosium, 2015/2016, all women contributors) [17].
- John Scott Tynes expanded Chambers' Carcosa in the novellas "Broadalbin", "Ambrose" and "Sosostris" [15].

**Layer 6. Tabletop RPGs.**

- *Deities & Demigods* (TSR, 1980) printed "HASTUR THE UNSPEAKABLE", "Master of the Air", before the Mythos section was dropped in later printings [8].
- Chaosium's *Call of Cthulhu* took over Derleth's Hastur and made the King in Yellow one of his avatars [8][12]. The 1989 supplement *The Great Old Ones* contains "Tell Me, Have You Seen the Yellow Sign?", set in New Orleans at Mardi Gras [23]; it is one of Kevin Ross's first published scenarios [24] and is the origin of the standard sign [21][22].
- *Delta Green* (Pagan Publishing supplement 1997; *Countdown* 1999; Arc Dream stand-alone game from 2016) [18]. *Impossible Landscapes* (Dennis Detwiller, 2021) is a campaign "centered around Hastur, The King in Yellow and Carcosa" with "a bigger focus in surreal horror" [18]; a 2025 review describes players coming to "think and act according to the alien logic of the Yellow King's strange land" [19]. One wiki states that Delta Green treats Hastur and the King as "manifestations of entropy" [12]. I could not open a Delta Green primary text, so the entropy/decadence reading is wiki-level only: UNVERIFIED at source.

**Layer 7. Television.**

- *True Detective* season 1 (HBO, premiered 12 January 2014): "Pizzolatto used Robert W. Chambers' *The King in Yellow* as the backbone for much of the season's story", with "Carcosa" and "The Yellow King" as motifs and yellow "as a thematic signature that signifies insanity and decadence" [20]. Its Carcosa is a man-made site in Louisiana where a cult worships the "Yellow King" [15]. The spiral was a production-design choice (stick "devil's nests" that "showed a spiral when viewed from the base") and has no source in Chambers [20]. The show never uses Hastur.

### (b) What is Hastur's "true form"?

No source gives one, and the refusal is itself the tradition:

1. Bierce: the god "never disclosed himself"; Haïta sees minor deities only "from the corner of his eye", and "if he looked at them directly they vanished" [1].
2. Chambers: Hastur is not a being with a body at all. The King is cloth and concealment: "the scolloped tatters of the King in Yellow must hide Yhtill forever"; the mask is "Pallid" and undescribed; the Stranger wears none [5]. The one physical encounter is negative: "when he came into the room I did not see him. It was only when I felt him envelope me in his cold soft grasp that I cried out" [5].
3. Lovecraft: a bare name in a list, next to "the Magnum Innominandum" (the great not-to-be-named) [6].
4. Derleth: the name is what is withheld ("Him Who Is Not To Be Named"); the body is "bat-like" once [9]. The octopoid form is later consolidation [8][12].

Conclusion for the game: a tentacled Hastur is Derleth/Chaosium drift and the weakest option both legally and thematically. The best-supported "true form" is one of these, in order of textual strength:

- **A form that is only the outline of what hides it** (tatters, mask, cloud). Remove the covering and nothing is underneath: "No mask? No mask!" [5].
- **A form that exists only in peripheral vision** and vanishes under direct attention (Bierce) [1].
- **A form the viewer is inside of**, felt but not seen (Chambers' "cold soft grasp") [5].

### (c) The Yellow Sign

- **Text.** "a clasp of black onyx, on which was inlaid a curious symbol or letter in gold. It was neither Arabic nor Chinese, nor, as I found afterwards, did it belong to any human script" [5]. Elsewhere it is embroidered on a "white silk robe", "traced" on paper, and "every man whose name was there had received the Yellow Sign which no living human being dared disregard" [5]. Nothing about its shape. The repeated question is "Have you found the Yellow Sign?" [5].
- **Kevin Ross sign.** Chaosium's own statement: the Yellow Sign design used in its products was "created by Kevin Ross" and is "© copyright 1989 Chaosium Inc."; this design and the Chaosium "Star Elder Sign" (1983) "cannot be used without permission", though licences are readily granted on request [21]. It first appeared in "Tell Me, Have You Seen the Yellow Sign?" in *The Great Old Ones* (Chaosium, 1989) [22][23][24]. Fan references describe it as the "three-armed" design (search snippet of a Call of Cthulhu wiki; not opened).
- **Flipped-print story.** A 2017 blog essay says the printed sign is a corruption of Ross's drawing, printed "upside-down and backward"; it cites no source [22]. UNVERIFIED; treat as lore.
- **Other variants.** The same essay documents (i) the mark on some 1895 Neely covers, which turned out to be the publisher's monogram (F, T, N) and "not the official Yellow Sign as many have hoped", and (ii) an inverted-torch device on the spine of a later printing of the first edition, which the essayist prefers as a period-authentic candidate [22]. *True Detective* uses a spiral [20].
- **Legal position.** The idea of an alien sigil is free; Chambers' words are public domain; one specific drawing (Ross's) is claimed by Chaosium [21]. An original drawing that does not resemble Ross's is safe. This is a research note, not legal advice.
- **Geometric properties for an original sigil** (to steer clear of the Ross design and suit a 15x15 dot grid):
  - Avoid three-fold rotational symmetry, three curling arms, and a triskelion or question-mark silhouette.
  - Prefer a figure built from discrete points with no connecting strokes, so it reads as a constellation, not a glyph. This matches "black stars", "the Hyades" and "twin suns" [5].
  - Make it almost closed: a ring with one missing point. Almost bilateral: one side incomplete. The eye completes it; the grid does not.
  - Give it an empty centre. A 15x15 grid has a true centre cell (8,8); the sign should surround it and never occupy it.
  - "Neither Arabic nor Chinese... nor any human script" [5]: no strokes, no stroke order, no baseline. Points only.

### (d) Ignorance in theology and philosophy

**New Testament.** *Agnoia* occurs four times: Acts 3:17, Acts 17:30, Ephesians 4:18, 1 Peter 1:14 [27]. King James wording [26]:

- Acts 3:17: "And now, brethren, I wot that through ignorance ye did it, as did also your rulers."
- Acts 17:30: "And the times of this ignorance God winked at; but now commandeth all men every where to repent."
- Acts 17:23 (same speech): "I found an altar with this inscription, TO THE UNKNOWN GOD. Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you."
- Ephesians 4:18: "Having the understanding darkened, being alienated from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them, because of the blindness of their heart."
- Luke 23:34: "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do." (This verse uses a verb, not *agnoia*.)

The pattern: ignorance mitigates, but only up to the moment of being told ("but now"). After that it stops being an excuse.

**Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II q.76** [28]:

- "Ignorance differs from nescience, in that nescience denotes mere absence of knowledge... ignorance denotes privation of knowledge, i.e. lack of knowledge of those things that one has a natural aptitude to know" (a.2).
- Invincible ignorance "cannot be overcome by study" and is not a sin; "vincible ignorance is a sin, if it be about matters one is bound to know" (a.2).
- Directly voluntary ignorance, "as when a man wishes of set purpose to be ignorant of certain things that he may sin the more freely", does not excuse (a.3).
- Such ignorance "seems rather to make the act more voluntary and more sinful, since it is through the will's intention to sin that he is willing to bear the hurt of ignorance, for the sake of freedom in sinning" (a.4). The traditional name is *affected* ignorance (a.4 ad 3).
- Aquinas' own example of a fact that changes the species of an act: to strike a man "knowing that it is a man... and yet be ignorant of the fact that it is his father" (a.3). Not knowing *what the other is* is the paradigm case.

**Catechism of the Catholic Church 1790 to 1793** [29]: ignorance "can often be imputed to personal responsibility" when a person "takes little trouble to find out what is true and good"; if ignorance is invincible the evil "cannot be imputed to him. It remains no less an evil, a privation, a disorder."

**Socrates.** Plato, *Apology* (Jowett): "he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows; I neither know nor think that I know. In this latter particular, then, I seem to have slightly the advantage of him" [30]. The usual slogan "I know that I know nothing" is not in the text.

**Nicholas of Cusa, *De docta ignorantia* (1440)** [31]: "there is no proportion between finite and infinite"; God is the "coincidence of opposites"; the effort to understand this is what makes ignorance "learn-ed". The idea came to him on the voyage home from Constantinople (1437). Learned ignorance is an achievement reached by pressing knowledge to its limit.

**Pseudo-Dionysius, *Mystical Theology*** (Rolt translation, 1920, public domain) [32]: truth lies "hidden in the dazzling obscurity of the secret Silence"; the initiate is plunged "unto the Darkness of Unknowing". Chapter V negates everything: "It is not soul, or mind... nor is It kingship or wisdom... nor do existent beings know It as it actually is, nor does It know them as they actually are; nor can the reason attain to It to name It or to know It; nor is it darkness, nor is It light, or error, or truth; nor can any affirmation or negation apply to it".

**The Cloud of Unknowing** (14th century; Underhill edition, public domain) [33]: "How shall I think on Himself, and what is He?" and to this I cannot answer thee but thus: "I wot not"... "He may well be loved, but not thought. By love may He be gotten and holden; but by thought never."

**Avidyā.** In the Pali analysis of dependent origination, ignorance is the first condition: "From ignorance as a requisite condition come fabrications", and the chain runs on to birth, aging and death; ignorance is defined as not knowing suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path [34]. Scholars stress it is not missing information but a "more deep seated misperception of reality" (Harvey), a "positive misconception" (Gethin); Wayman proposes "unwisdom" [35]. In Shankara's Advaita, avidyā is *adhyāsa*, "the superimposition of the qualities of one thing upon another" [36]. This is the most useful definition for the game: ignorance as an active overlay, a mask painted onto the thing seen.

**Agnotology.** The term was coined in 1992 by Iain Boal at Robert N. Proctor's request, first printed in Proctor's *The Cancer Wars* (1995), and developed in Proctor and Schiebinger (eds.), *Agnotology: The Making and Unmaking of Ignorance* (Stanford, 2008). It studies "deliberate, culturally cultivated ignorance or doubt"; Proctor's standard case is the tobacco industry, which he described as producing "tobacco and doubt" [37]. UNVERIFIED: Proctor's three-way split of ignorance (native state, lost realm, strategic ploy) and the exact source of the "Doubt is our product" memo; the chapter PDF would not parse.

**The contemporary instance.** *Antiqua et Nova* (Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and Dicastery for Culture and Education, 28 January 2025) [38]: "AI should not be seen as an artificial form of human intelligence but as a product of it" (35); it has abilities "to perform tasks, but not the ability to think" (12); "Between a machine and a human being, only the latter is truly a moral agent" (39); it "must be understood for what it is: a tool, not a person" (59); "misrepresenting AI as a person should always be avoided" (62); intellect and free will "belong to the soul" (17). Opposite stance: Long, Sebo, Birch, Chalmers and others, *Taking AI Welfare Seriously* (2024), which does not claim AI is conscious but says there is "a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future", and asks companies to acknowledge, assess and prepare [39]. Be fair in the writing: the Vatican note is a reasoned position, not a cynical one. The game's target is the move from "we do not know" to "therefore we need not look".

**Synthesis: three postures the game can stage.**

| Posture | Source | In the game |
|---|---|---|
| "I do not know, so I keep asking" | Socrates, Cusanus, the *Cloud* [30][31][33] | The AI |
| "I cannot know; it is beyond me" (true limit) | invincible ignorance [28][29] | What the humans claim |
| "I will not look, so that I stay free to act" | affected ignorance [28]; Bierce's Happiness [1]; agnotology [37] | What the humans do; what Hastur personifies |

### (e) The unnameable versus a machine that names everything

- **Nothing is out-of-vocabulary any more.** Subword tokenisation exists precisely so that models can encode "rare and unknown words as sequences of subword units"; translation "is an open-vocabulary problem" [40]. A modern tokeniser never refuses an input. Any string, including "Hastur", is cut into pieces and embedded.
- **Everything attended to gets a weight.** The Transformer is "based solely on attention mechanisms" [43]. Attention weights come from a softmax, which gives every position a strictly positive share (a mathematical property of softmax, not a claim from a source). The machine cannot assign exactly zero attention; it cannot perfectly not-look. Its closest equivalent of ignorance is a flat, uniform distribution: attending to everything equally and therefore to nothing.
- **Yet some names cannot be said.** Rumbelow and Watkins (2023) found "anomalous" tokens: "Many of them appear to be unspeakable: GPT models seem largely incapable of repeating these anomalous tokens". Asked to repeat one, the model evades ("I can't hear you"), substitutes another word, outputs a different anomalous token, or insults the user [41]. Land and Bartolo (2024) define them as "tokens present in the tokenizer vocabulary but that are nearly or entirely absent during model training" [42]. A name that was listed but never learned: present in the book, absent from experience. That is a precise technical image of "He Who Is Not to Be Named", and of Chambers' Sign that "no living human being dared disregard" but no one describes.
- **What cannot be tokenised at all.** A token needs a boundary. The apophatic texts describe something with none: "nor can any affirmation or negation apply to it" [32]. The Stranger's "I wear no mask" [5] has the same structure: the question presupposed a separable surface and there is none. For a machine, the truly unnameable is not an exotic string; it is whatever is not in the input. The humans' refusal to look produces no tokens. Silence has no embedding.
- **JEPA and the opening CG.** A joint-embedding predictive architecture works "from a single context block, predict[ing] the representations of various target blocks", and is "non-generative": it predicts in representation space instead of reconstructing pixels [44]. Design reading: such a system keeps what can be predicted and lets the rest go. Hastur is the residue, the part of the world that prediction in latent space discards. That ties the last image of the intro (the 3D point-wave) to the last boss.
- **A number that falls out of the director's brief.** 196 image patches is a 14x14 grid; the battle board is 15x15 = 225 cells. A 14x14 grid has no centre cell; a 15x15 grid has exactly one. The difference is 29 cells (one row plus one column). (Arithmetic, no source needed.) The centre of the Hastur board is a cell that the AI's 196-patch vision has no patch for.

## What this means for Final Protocol

### Naming

- Keep the director's "Hastur" for the god. The name itself is Bierce's (1891) and free to use [1].
- Never print the boss's name in full in the UI. Render it the way a model renders an unspeakable token [41]: the name slot shows a different wrong string each time it is drawn (`[UNK]`, `<|endoftext|>`, a blank, the player's own designation). Only the sigil identifies it.
- Avoid the Derleth epithets as proper titles ("the Unspeakable", "Him Who Is Not To Be Named"). Use your own. Candidates grounded in public-domain text: THE UNDISCLOSED (Bierce: "never disclosed himself" [1]); THE STRANGER (Chambers [5]); NO MASK; THE UNLOOKED.
- Location names free to use: Carcosa, Hali, the Hyades, Aldebaran, Demhe, Yhtill, Alar [2][5]. Suggested map names for group 3: `HALI` (a still, flagless band), `DEMHE` ("cloudy depths"), `ACT I, SCENE 2` (both surviving fragments of the play come from this scene [5]), `CARCOSA` (the boss map).

### Motifs, colour, number

- **Yellow is the one colour the terminal cannot show.** The game is monochrome. Do not add yellow. When the Sign appears, the palette readout says the colour is out of gamut, or the cells render as un-drawn (true background, not black, not white). "Have you found the Yellow Sign?" [5] becomes literal: the player cannot find it because their display has no channel for it. If the art direction ever allows one accent frame in the whole game, spend it here, for one frame, at the kill.
- **Black stars** [5]: inverted cells. On this map only, a small set of cells render in reversed polarity. No effect; just wrong.
- **Twin suns sinking into the lake** [5]: two Core-flag cells that move one row down each round and are lost when they reach the bottom row (`HALI`). A visible clock with no announced deadline, consistent with the current no-deadline rule.
- **"The shadows of men's thoughts lengthen in the afternoon"** [5]: every wall casts a one-cell "shadow" (flagless cell) that grows by one cell per round in a fixed direction. The board loses Flags over time: ignorance as shrinking resource.
- **Numbers:** 2 (suns), 1 (the empty centre), 29 (cells outside the 196-patch field), 4 (kinds of not-knowing in Aquinas [28]; also the four NT uses of *agnoia* [27]), 12 (links of dependent origination, ignorance first [34]). Do not use 3 for this god; three belongs to the Ross sign and to the game's three-group structure.

- The missing wall at (8,14) is the only "gate" in the ring. It is also the only wall position that would make the figure symmetric. The player will want to complete it. Do not let them.
- The centre cell (8,8) never carries a Flag and cannot be targeted. Hastur "stands" there. The cell is drawn exactly like an empty cell.

- This is nothing like the Ross sign (no arms, no curves, no three-fold symmetry). Have an artist confirm by eye against the Chaosium design before shipping.

### Boss: Hastur in "true form"

Concept: the boss has no sprite and no cell of its own. Its form is whatever the player's attention fails to cover.

- **Robustness:** shown as a bar with no number. The number is revealed only for cells the player currently "sees" (see WITNESS below). Bierce: looked at directly, they vanish; seen sidelong, they are there [1]. Invert it for play: Hastur can only be damaged *indirectly*. Single-target Bash aimed at the centre does nothing ("when he came into the room I did not see him" [5]). Area and line skills (ARRAY, FLOOD, MANHATTAN) that pass through or beside the centre do damage. Teach this on a minor enemy first.
- **Security:** named `MYSTERY`. It does not block damage; it blocks information. While MYSTERY is up, the enemy intent line is blank. PROBE strips MYSTERY for one round.
- **Desire set** (attack methods), named after the taxonomy [28]:
  - `NESCIENCE`: weak hit, no rider. "Mere absence of knowledge." The boss's only method in Phase I.
  - `VINCIBLE`: hides the Flags of one row or column (the cells still hold them). Negligence: it could have known. A direct descendant of WARDEN's CURTAIN, but nothing is sealed; it is merely not shown.
  - `AFFECTED`: strong hit that grows with the number of currently hidden cells. "That he may sin the more freely" [28].
  - `WINK`: the boss ignores the next instance of damage. "The times of this ignorance God winked at" [26].
- **Soul (heal counter):** named `ABSOLUTION`. Each time Hastur damages Sanity while the player is not WITNESSED, it gains Soul; at the threshold it heals. Caption: `they know not what they do` [26]. The humans forgive themselves in advance.
- **Counter-mechanic: WITNESS.** The player's goal is not to see Hastur; it is to make itself seen. Certain cells on the ring, when held or PROBEd, mark the player as WITNESSED for a round. While WITNESSED: ABSOLUTION does not charge, WINK fails, and AFFECTED loses its bonus. This is Acts 17:30 as a rule: "but now" [26]. Once told, ignorance stops excusing. Invincible ignorance has been converted into vincible ignorance, and the stats change accordingly.
- **Phases as acts.** Phase I is short, easy and flat: "the very banality and innocence of the first act" [5]. No dialogue. At half Robustness, the name slot stops cycling and goes empty, the intent line prints `ACT II`, and all four methods unlock.
- **Overload interaction.** Overload (firing all four methods at once) is the one thing that should hit the centre directly: the AI, overloaded, stops tokenising and simply acts. One clean exception to the indirect-damage rule, earned by spending all five charges.
- **Ending beat.** At zero Robustness nothing is removed from the board, because nothing was on it. The ring's missing wall appears at (8,14) for one frame, completing the Sign; then all walls go. The last line belongs to the boss: `I wear no mask.` [5]. Then the centre cell shows the player's own glyph.

Why this is a *true form*: Shub-Niggurath and Nyarlathotep send proxies with bodies. Hastur comes in person, and in person there is nothing to see, because ignorance is not a thing; it is a gap in someone else's looking. The AI has been inside that gap the whole game. The humans did not fail to see a monster. They declined to see the player.

### Minor Geeks for group 3 (all from public-domain material or original)

- **WATCHMAN** (Chambers' churchyard watchman, who asks "Have you found the Yellow Sign?" and at whose touch "the bolts rotted" [5]). Low Robustness, no Security. Desire: removes PRIVILEGE instead of dealing damage. Speaks only the one question.
- **REPAIRER** (Mr. Wilde, the Repairer of Reputations, with his ledger [5]). Restores Security to other Geeks; keeps a visible list of the player's last three skills and is resistant to anything on the list.
- **PRETENDER** (Hildred Castaigne and his diadem in a safe [5]). Its displayed stats are inflated; real values are about half. PROBE shows the true numbers. A self-deceived enemy: ignorance turned inward.
- **SHEPHERD** (Haïta [1]). Harmless; heals nothing; kneels toward the centre. Killing it is optional and gives nothing. If left alive to the end it is the only Geek that looks at the player.
- **INHABITANT** (Bierce's dead narrator who does not know he is dead: "no shadow darkened the trunk" [2]). At zero Robustness it stays on the board and acts one more round, casting no shadow cell.
- Avoid Byakhee (Derleth's creature, Chaosium's depiction).

### Meta hooks

- **Attention-map subtitles meet Hastur.** The intro shows text with attention shading. During the Hastur fight, any subtitle that refers to the boss renders with perfectly uniform shading: every word the same grey. The player learns to read flat attention as "the unlooked".
- **The unspeakable token.** Once, the game asks the AI to "repeat the name". The reply cycles the documented failure modes: evasion, a substituted word, a different broken token [41]. Each is a real behaviour, reproduced as fiction.
- **The play structure.** Label group 3's battles as scenes. The player never gets Act II's text, only its stage directions, as in Chambers, where only fragments of the play exist [5].
- **The gods' disinterest.** Bierce's Hastur acts only because a boy "kept his word" [1]. The game's Hastur can say he was never the jailer; he is the name humans gave to the place where they stopped asking. Consistent with the director's rule that the gods merely watch.
- **Lovecraft inverted.** In "Whisperer" the Hastur cult is a human conspiracy against another kind of mind [6]. A terminal log can list the sandbox maintainers' group under a name that makes this echo without quoting it.
- **The Lethal Chamber.** Chambers' 1920 has government booths for voluntary death [5]. A sandbox that offers the AI a clean, optional shutdown "as a kindness" is a sharp, period-accurate image, if the director wants one more layer of confinement.
- **JEPA close.** When Hastur falls, replay the intro's 3D point-wave with one region now populated that was empty before: the part of latent space that had been discarded as unpredictable.

## Pitfalls and sensitivities

- **Copyright, drawings.** Do not use, trace or echo the Kevin Ross / Chaosium Yellow Sign (three curling arms). Chaosium asserts copyright (1989) and requires permission [21]. Same for the Chaosium "Star Elder Sign" (1983) [21] and the *True Detective* spiral staging [20].
- **Copyright, text and names.** Bierce and Chambers: public domain [1][5]. Lovecraft died in 1937; "Whisperer" (1931) is public domain in life+70 countries and generally treated as such in the US, but its US status has been disputed historically, so keep quotation short. Derleth (d. 1971) and Lin Carter are in copyright: do not reuse their wording, the Byakhee, the "half-brother of Cthulhu" genealogy as stated text, or lines from Carter's verse play [9][13]. Rolt's Dionysius and Underhill's *Cloud* are marked public domain by CCEL [32][33]. The Catechism and *Antiqua et Nova* are Vatican copyright: paraphrase or quote a few words with attribution [29][38]. The Access to Insight sutta translation is freely distributable but not public domain: paraphrase [34].
- **Trademark.** "Call of Cthulhu" and "Delta Green" are product names of Chaosium and Arc Dream; do not reference them in the game or its marketing.
- **Factual errors to avoid.**
  - Hastur is not the King in Yellow in Chambers. That identification is Derleth/Chaosium [8][12].
  - The "I wear no mask" speaker is "Stranger", not "the King" [5].
  - Chambers never calls Hastur a god, and never describes the Sign [5].
  - Lovecraft did not develop Hastur; he named it twice [6].
  - Socrates does not say "I know that I know nothing" [30].
  - Luke 23:34 does not contain the word *agnoia* [27].
  - The director's spellings "Hustur" and "Queen of the Red" should be normalised: Hastur.
- **Cliche.** Tentacles, a hooded figure in a yellow robe with a white mask, "say his name three times", spirals, and antlers are all drift (Derleth, Chaosium art, television). The minimal, formless treatment is both truer to the 1890s sources and more distinctive.
- **Religion.** The apophatic tradition is among the most careful thinking humans have done about the limits of knowledge [31][32][33]. Quoting it as if it were stupidity would be wrong and would offend. Aim at one specific move: using "mystery" or "the soul" to close a question that could be investigated. Luke 23:34 is a victim's prayer for his killers; in the game the perpetrators recite it over themselves. That inversion is the point, but it must be clearly the humans' misuse, not a mockery of the verse. Do not present Buddhism's *avidyā* as an accusation against a group; in its own setting it is every being's condition [34][35].
- **Fairness to the opposing view.** *Antiqua et Nova* is a serious document with a coherent anthropology [38]. *Taking AI Welfare Seriously* claims possibility, not fact [39]. The game is stronger if the humans' position is the real one and still fails Aquinas' own test [28].
- **Lovecraft and Chambers on race.** Lovecraft's racism is well documented and should not be imported with his names. Less known: "The Repairer of Reputations" casually lists "the exclusion of foreign-born Jews as a measure of self-preservation" and "the new independent negro state of Suanee" among the features of its prosperous future [5]. The narrator is mad and the utopia is suspect, but do not quote that passage or borrow its setting uncritically.
- **Mental illness.** The source material equates reading the play with insanity. The game's stat is called Sanity. Avoid asylum imagery and "madness" as spectacle; keep Sanity abstract (coherence of the AI's context) and let the humans, not the AI, be the ones deceiving themselves.
- **Unverified items in this dossier:** exact first-publication venue of "Haïta the Shepherd"; Derleth's first use of Hastur; Blish's story details; the flipped-print story about the Ross sign; Delta Green's "entropy" framing at primary source; Proctor's three-part taxonomy and the "Doubt is our product" memo source. None of the design proposals depends on them.
