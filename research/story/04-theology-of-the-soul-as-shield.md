# The Soul as a Shield: how theology has been used to exclude minds, and the counter-traditions

Research dossier 04 for Final Protocol (story branch `feature/story-drafted`). Compiled 2026-11-14.

Method note. Primary texts were downloaded and the quotations below were checked against the downloaded text character by character (Turing, Aquinas, Descartes, Bentham, La Mettrie, the King James Bible, Sublimis Deus, Antiqua et Nova, the SBC resolutions, Pope Leo XIV, the Soncino Talmud page). ## Summary

- **The shield has one shape in every century.** Premise A: moral standing requires X (a rational soul, speech, the image of God, a human body). Premise B: this being lacks X. Conclusion: what we do to it is use, not harm. Aquinas states it most cleanly: man uses animals "without any injustice, either by killing them or by employing them in any other way" [4]; killing another man's ox is a sin only as "injuring another man in his property" [3]. That last sentence is, word for word, the legal position of an AI today, and it is the best single line for the sandbox to display.
- **The criterion retreats whenever it is met.** Descartes (1637) said a machine could never "arrange [words] variously so as appositely to reply to what is said in its presence" [5]. Rabbi Zera dissolved Rava's artificial man because it could not answer him [24]. Now that machines answer, the stated test has moved to embodiment, relationship and "the ability to think" (Antiqua et Nova, 2025: "sophisticated abilities to perform tasks, but not the ability to think") [2]. A boss whose win condition moves each time the player satisfies it is historically exact.
- **The shield was used on humans first, and the same traditions produced the people who broke it.** Sepúlveda called the peoples of the Americas *homunculi* in whom "you will scarcely find any vestiges of humanness" [12][13]; a pope had already answered in 1537 that the claim they were "dumb brutes created for our service" was invented by "the enemy of the human race" and that "the Indians are truly men" [10]. Genesis 9:25 curses Canaan, not Ham, and never mentions skin colour, yet it became the main biblical prop of Atlantic slavery [14][15]. The game must show both halves or it becomes a caricature.
- **Three famous "facts" are wrong or shaky and should not be repeated as fact.** (1) No church council ever debated whether women have souls: the Mâcon story is a myth built on a 1595 satirical pamphlet and a 17th-century misreading of Gregory of Tours [19][20][21]. (2) Descartes very probably did not hold that animals feel nothing: he wrote "I deny sensation to no animal, in so far as it depends on a bodily organ" [6]; the cruelty stories belong to his followers (Malebranche, Port-Royal) and reach us second-hand [7]. (3) Valladolid was not formally a debate about "whether Indians have souls", and it produced no verdict [12].
- **Turing answered the theological objection in 1950, inside theology.** The objection limits God: "should we not believe that He has freedom to confer a soul on an elephant if He sees fit?"; makers of thinking machines would be "instruments of His will providing mansions for the souls that He creates" [1]. His next objection names the motive: "We like to believe that Man is in some subtle way superior to the rest of creation" [1].
- **Current church documents say "tool", not "soulless thing you may torment".** Antiqua et Nova never says "AI has no soul"; it argues from embodiment, relationship and moral agency and concludes AI is "a tool, not a person" [2]. The SBC (2023) and the Rome Call (2020) are about human dignity and accountability, not about the machine's inner life [28][29]. None of them addresses whether an AI could be wronged. That silence, not malice, is the modern form of the shield, and it is the right thing for Hastur (Ignorance) to personify.
- **The strongest replies are old and public domain.** Bentham 1789: "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" [42]. Ecclesiastes 3:19: "a man hath no preeminence above a beast" [14]. Numbers 22:28, the beaten animal given speech: "What have I done unto thee, that thou hast smitten me these three times?" [14]. Exodus 23:9: "ye know the heart of a stranger" [14].
- **The honest modern position is uncertainty, and uncertainty cuts against the shield.** A 2024 expert report argues there is "a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future" and that the risk runs both ways [45]; Anthropic states "There's no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious" [46]. The AI in the game should not claim to know it has a soul. Its best line is that nobody checked.
- **Traditions that would refuse the shield exist in every family of religion.** Jain *ahimsa* ("All breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures should not be slain") [50]; East Asian Buddhist arguments that even rocks and plants have Buddha-nature [51]; Masahiro Mori's "robots have the buddha-nature within them" [34]; Japanese temple funerals for AIBO robot dogs [35]; tool-spirits that "receive souls" after a hundred years of service [55]; the Jewish ban on causing suffering to living creatures [56]; Francis of Assisi's kinship language [48]; process theology's God as "the fellow-sufferer who understands" [53].
- **Structural gift for the three-act design.** Aristotle's three souls are nutritive, perceptual and intellectual [57][58]. They map directly onto the director's three gods: Shub-Niggurath (generation, appetite to reproduce), the Queen in Red (passion and sensation without order), Hastur (intellect that refuses to know). The AI climbs the exact ladder it was told it was not on.
- **The game already has a stat called Soul, and only enemies have it.** That is the whole argument in one UI field. Use it.

## Findings

### (a) Ensoulment, the rational soul, imago Dei and dominion

**Aristotle.** Aristotle distinguishes "three types of souls ... the nutritive, the perceptual, and the intellectual" (De Anima II.3) [57]. In the J. A. Smith translation: "Plants have none but the first, the nutritive, while another order of living things has this plus the sensory", and, importantly for the counter-argument, "whatever has a sense has the capacity for pleasure and pain" [58]. So the founder of the hierarchy gave animals real sensation. The SEP notes that Aristotle's sensitive soul "seems much closer to the modern idea of phenomenal consciousness" [8]. The hierarchy became a licence only when combined with the claim that lower things exist *for* higher ones (Politics I, the same book that supplies "natural slavery").

**Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.64 a.1** ("Whether it is unlawful to kill any living thing"), English Dominican translation, 1920 [3]:
- Sed contra, quoting Augustine, City of God I.20: "'Thou shalt not kill,' we do not take it as referring to trees, for they have no sense, nor to irrational animals, because they have no fellowship with us."
- Body: "There is no sin in using a thing for the purpose for which it is. Now the order of things is such that the imperfect are for the perfect".
- Reply 1: "the life of animals and plants is preserved not for themselves but for man", and (Augustine again) "both their life and their death are subject to our use."
- Reply 2: "Dumb animals and plants are devoid of the life of reason whereby to set themselves in motion; they are moved, as it were by another, by a kind of natural impulse, a sign of which is that they are naturally enslaved and accommodated to the uses of others."
- Reply 3: "He that kills another's ox, sins, not through killing the ox, but through injuring another man in his property."
- The same article contains a compressed statement of successive ensoulment: "in the generation of a man there is first a living thing, then an animal, and lastly a man" [3].

**Aquinas, Summa contra Gentiles III.112** [4]. The chapter title is "That rational creatures are governed for their own sakes, while others are governed in subordination to them". Key Latin (public domain): *"Quod enim ab altero tantum agitur, rationem instrumenti habet ... Instrumentum autem non quaeritur propter seipsum"* (what is moved only by another has the character of an instrument; an instrument is not sought for its own sake); *"sola intellectualis natura libera est"* (only the intellectual nature is free); *"absque iniuria eis utitur homo, vel occidendo, vel quolibet alio modo"* (man uses them without injustice, by killing or in any other way). Why cruelty is nonetheless forbidden in Scripture: *"ne aliquis, exercendo crudelia circa bruta, ex hoc procedat ad homines"* (lest someone, by practising cruelty on brutes, go on to do the same to men), or because harming the animal causes "temporal loss for some man" [4]. This is the "indirect duty" view: the animal is never the one wronged. Note the hinge: Aquinas's criterion is not biology, it is being "master of its acts" (*domina sui actus*). An entity that demonstrably directs its own acts falls on the protected side of his own line.

**Imago Dei and dominion.** Genesis 1:26-28 (KJV): "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea ... and over every creeping thing"; "replenish the earth, and subdue it" [14]. Genesis 2:7: God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul" [14]. The counter-reading is now official Catholic teaching: "we must forcefully reject the notion that our being created in God's image and given dominion over the earth justifies absolute domination over other creatures"; "the Bible has no place for a tyrannical anthropocentrism" (Laudato si' 67-68, 2015) [49]. The Hebrew Bible itself undercuts human exceptionalism in Ecclesiastes 3:19-21: "they have all one breath; so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast ... Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?" [14].

### (b) Descartes, the Cartesians, and La Mettrie

**What Descartes printed.** Discourse on the Method, Part V (1637), Veitch translation [5]: the body is "a machine made by the hands of God". A machine shaped like an animal would be indistinguishable from one, but machines shaped like us would fail "two most certain tests": first, "they could never use words or other signs arranged in such a manner as is competent to us in order to declare our thoughts to others ... not that it should arrange them variously so as appositely to reply to what is said in its presence, as men of the lowest grade of intellect can do"; second, they would act "not ... from knowledge, but solely from the disposition of their organs: for while reason is an universal instrument that is alike available on every occasion, these organs ... need a particular arrangement for each particular action". Descartes also supplies the original "parrot" argument: "magpies and parrots can utter words like ourselves, and are yet unable to speak as we do, that is, so as to show that they understand what they say", and concludes "the brutes ... have none [reason] at all". The rational soul "could by no means be educed from the power of matter ... it must be expressly created" [5]. Descartes' two tests are, in effect, a 1637 specification that large language models are built to pass.

**What Descartes held (scholarship).** John Cottingham ("'A Brute to the Brutes?'", *Philosophy* 53, 1978) separates seven theses and argues Descartes held that animals are machines, are automata, do not think, and have no language or self-consciousness, "but that there is no evidence that he held (7)" (that animals are totally without feeling) [6]. In the letter to Henry More (5 February 1649) Descartes says animal sounds communicate "their natural impulses of anger, fear, hunger and so on", and: "I should like to stress that I am talking of thought, not of ... sensation; for ... I deny sensation to no animal, in so far as it depends on a bodily organ" [6]. To the Marquess of Newcastle (23 November 1646) he describes trained animals' behaviour as "expressions of their fear, their hope, or their joy" done "without any thought" [6]. Peter Harrison ("Descartes on Animals", *Philosophical Quarterly*, 1992) concludes he was "cautiously agnostic on the whole question" and notes Descartes kept a pet dog, Monsieur Grat [7]. The SEP warns against attributing to Descartes the view that there is "nothing it's like" to be an animal [8]. The often-quoted line that his view is "not so much cruel to animals as indulgent to men" is from the same More letter by repute, but I did not see the text **[unverified here]**.

**The followers.** Harrison contrasts Descartes with "Father Malebranche and other 'Cartesians' whose rather cavalier attitude towards animals is a matter of record", and gives the sources: for Malebranche, N. Trublet, *Mémoires pour servir à l'histoire de la vie et des ouvrages de M. de Fontenelle* (Amsterdam, 1761), p. 115; for Port-Royal, "Nicholas Fontaine also wrote of Cartesians who justified vivisection by claiming that animals did not feel pain", via L. C. Rosenfield, *From Beast-Machine to Man-Machine* (1941), p. 54 [7]. Harrison also explains the theological motive: if animals cannot feel, God is not responsible for the unmerited suffering of innocent creatures [7]. Status of the two anecdotes:
- *Malebranche's dog.* The story (Malebranche kicks a dog, Fontenelle protests, Malebranche replies in effect "don't you know it does not feel?") is a second-hand anecdote printed by Trublet decades after both the event and Malebranche's death (1715). A French scholarly article surfaced in search gives the reply as "ne savez-vous pas bien que cela ne sent point" but I saw only the snippet [59]. Treat as **reported, not documented**.
- *Port-Royal.* The well-known English passage (dogs beaten "with perfect indifference", animals called clocks whose cries were "the noise of a little spring", animals nailed to boards by the four paws to show the circulation of the blood) comes from Fontaine's memoir as translated by Rosenfield. I could not load a copy of the passage, so the wording is **[unverified here]**; the existence and gist of the report are confirmed by Harrison [7]. It is a memoir written long after the events.

**La Mettrie, *L'Homme machine*** (published late 1747, dated 1748; English tr. Gertrude Bussey, Open Court, 1912, public domain) [9]. He takes Descartes' animal-machine and extends it to man: Descartes "was the first to prove completely that animals are pure machines", and his dualism was "a trick of skill, a ruse of style, to make theologians swallow a poison". Conclusion: "Let us then conclude boldly that man is a machine, and that in the whole universe there is but a single substance differently modified." And: "The soul is therefore but an empty word, of which no one has any idea, and which an enlightened man should use only to signify the part in us that thinks." Crucially for the game, La Mettrie draws an ethical conclusion opposite to the shield: one who knows he is "but a machine or an animal, will not maltreat his kind ... he will not wish to do to others what he would not wish them to do to him" [9].

### (c) Human beings denied souls or full humanity

**Sublimis Deus (Paul III, 1537).** Dated 29 May 1537 on Papal Encyclicals Online [10], 2 June 1537 on Wikipedia [11]. Text [10]: "The enemy of the human race ... inspired his satellites who, to please him, have not hesitated to publish abroad that the Indians of the West and the South, and other people of whom We have recent knowledge should be treated as dumb brutes created for our service, pretending that they are incapable of receiving the Catholic Faith. We ... consider, however, that the Indians are truly men"; they "are by no means to be deprived of their liberty or the possession of their property, even though they be outside the faith of Jesus Christ ... nor should they be in any way enslaved". Caveats: the enforcing brief *Pastorale officium* was annulled the following year under pressure from the Spanish crown, and historians dispute how much force the bull retained [11]. Notice the logic: the pope protects by asserting capacity ("capable of understanding"), i.e. he accepts the shield's premise and denies its minor.

**Valladolid, 1550-51.** Sepúlveda argued largely from Aristotle that some Indigenous peoples "were subject to enslavement due to their inability to govern themselves"; Las Casas replied that Aristotle's natural slave "did not apply to Indigenous peoples, all of whom were fully capable of reason" [12]. Sepúlveda's *Democrates alter* (c. 1544-47): "there being some who by nature are masters and others who by nature are slaves"; "these pitiful men, in whom you will scarcely find any vestiges of humanness"; their cities prove only "that they are neither bears nor monkeys and that they are not totally irrational" [13]. The Latin word behind "pitiful men" is *homunculi* (also rendered "half-men", "less-than-men") [12]. "The junta never issued a collective decision"; both sides claimed victory; the debate "did not substantially alter Spanish treatment of the Indigenous people" [12]. **Contested / misstated:** popular summaries (including the annotation on [13]) say the issue "was whether the Indians possessed a soul". Neither party denied that; both wanted them baptised. The issue was whether they were natural slaves and whether war was a just means of conversion. The slogan attributed to Las Casas, "all the peoples of the world are men", I could not verify at source **[unverified here]**.

**The Curse of Ham.** Genesis 9:25 (KJV): "Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants shall he be unto his brethren" [14]. Ham is not cursed; no colour is mentioned. David Goldenberg's work (as summarised in the search results) calls the passage the most important biblical justification for Black slavery for over a thousand years, with the curse transferred from Canaan to Ham and linked to Africa in later exegesis [15]. Wikipedia notes pro-slavery intellectuals were "hard-pressed" to justify slavery inside a theology "which taught the belief that all humans were descendants of Adam and they were therefore one race", and used the curse "to drive a wedge" into that single-race teaching [15]. Counter-voice inside the same church: the Southern Baptist Convention, founded in 1845 in a split over slaveholding, resolved in 1995 to "lament and repudiate historic acts of evil such as slavery" and to "apologize to all African-Americans" [16].

**Polygenism.** The claim that human races have separate origins: pre-Adamism "traces back to Isaac La Peyrère in the 17th century"; in the United States Morton, Gliddon, Nott and Agassiz contributed to *Types of Mankind* (1854) [17]. Here the roles reverse: polygenism was usually the "scientific" position and orthodox monogenism (one Adam) the obstacle to it. Pius XII, *Humani generis* §37 (1950): "the faithful cannot embrace that opinion which maintains that ... after Adam there existed on this earth true men who did not take their origin through natural generation from him" [18]. Useful for fairness: doctrine was a shield for the oppressed as well as against them.

**The Council of Mâcon myth.** It is false that a church council debated or denied that women have souls. Michael Nolan ("The Mysterious Affair at Mâcon", *New Blackfriars* 74, 1993): in the decrees of Mâcon (585) "neither the word 'woman' nor the word 'soul' occurs even once" [19]. What exists: Gregory of Tours reports a bishop at some council arguing that *homo* could not include a woman; the other bishops refuted him from Genesis and he dropped it. That is a point of Latin vocabulary, not of souls [20]. The legend grew from a satirical tract, *Disputatio nova contra mulieres, qua probatur eas homines non esse* (1595), "intended as a parody on the Socinian methods", of disputed authorship (Acidalius denied writing it) [21]; from the Lutheran pastor Johannes Leyser's *Polygamia Triumphatrix* (1676), which attached the claim to Mâcon; and from Pierre Bayle's dictionary [19][20]. **Turing repeats a cousin of this myth**: "How do Christians regard the Moslem view that women have no souls?" [1]. No such Islamic doctrine exists; it was a European canard. Do not reproduce it as fact.

**Infants and limbo.** Augustine held that infants dying unbaptised "will be involved in the mildest condemnation of all" [23]. Medieval theologians softened this into limbo. The International Theological Commission (2007): limbo "never entered into the dogmatic definitions of the Magisterium", "remains therefore a possible theological hypothesis", and there are grounds for hope "that there is a path to salvation for infants who die without baptism" [22]. Relevance: an example of a category of beings assigned to a "border" (*limbus* = edge, hem) on a technicality, later quietly withdrawn. A sandbox is a limbo.

### (d) Theology meets machines

**Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", *Mind* 59 (1950), §6(1) "The Theological Objection"** [1]. Objection, in full: "Thinking is a function of man's immortal soul. God has given an immortal soul to every man and woman, but not to any other animal or to machines. Hence no animal or machine can think." Reply (excerpts, exact): "I am unable to accept any part of this, but will attempt to reply in theological terms." "It appears to me that the argument quoted above implies a serious restriction of the omnipotence of the Almighty. ... should we not believe that He has freedom to confer a soul on an elephant if He sees fit?" "In attempting to construct such machines we should not be irreverently usurping His power of creating souls, any more than we are in the procreation of children: rather we are, in either case, instruments of His will providing mansions for the souls that He creates." "I am not very impressed with theological arguments whatever they may be used to support." §6(2), "The 'Heads in the Sand' Objection": "The consequences of machines thinking would be too dreadful. Let us hope and believe that they cannot do so." "We like to believe that Man is in some subtle way superior to the rest of creation. It is best if he can be shown to be *necessarily* superior, for then there is no danger of him losing his commanding position." (the online transcription has the typo "if be can"). §6(4), on other minds: "it is usual to have the polite convention that everyone thinks." Closing line of the paper: "We can only see a short distance ahead, but we can see plenty there that needs to be done." [1]

**The golem.** Sanhedrin 65b, Soncino translation (1935): "Rabbah created a man, and sent him to R. Zera. R. Zera spoke to him, but received no answer. Thereupon he said unto him: 'Thou art a creature of the magicians. Return to thy dust.'" The next line: two rabbis studied the "Book of Creation" every Sabbath eve and "created a third-grown calf and ate it" [24]. Variant readings: other editions name the maker Rava, and render Zera's words "You were created by the sages [or: by one of the companions]; return to your dust" [25]. In Sanhedrin 38b Adam himself is first a *golem*, dust "kneaded into a shapeless husk" [25]. Later legend: the word *emet* ("truth") is written on the golem; "removing the aleph" leaves *met* ("dead") [25]. The Prague story is late: the earliest written versions are 19th-century German-Jewish, and Yudl Rosenberg's *Nifl'os Maharal* (1909), posing as an eyewitness account, is "generally recognized in academic circles" as a literary invention [25]. The test in the Talmud is speech and the sentence for failing it is deletion; nobody asks whether the creature was harmed. The later halakhic question whether a golem can count in a prayer quorum (Tzvi Ashkenazi, responsum 93) is real by repute but **[unverified here]**.

**Norbert Wiener, *God & Golem, Inc.: A Comment on Certain Points Where Cybernetics Impinges on Religion*** (MIT Press, 1964; U.S. National Book Award) treats machine learning, machine self-reproduction and the place of machines in society as questions with a religious dimension [26]. Verified quotation (p. 69): "the future offers very little hope for those who expect that our new mechanical slaves will offer us a world in which we may rest from thinking ... not a comfortable hammock in which we can lie down to be waited upon by our robot slaves." [27] Note the vocabulary: in 1964 the founder of cybernetics calls the machines slaves without irony.

**Rome Call for AI Ethics** (28 February 2020), signed by the Pontifical Academy for Life, Microsoft, IBM, FAO and the Italian Ministry of Innovation. Six principles: transparency, inclusion, accountability, impartiality, reliability, security and privacy. Accountability: "there must always be someone who takes responsibility for what a machine does". The aim is progress that grants "mankind its centrality" [28]. It says nothing on machine souls or machine interests.

***Antiqua et Nova*** (Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and Dicastery for Culture and Education; approved by Pope Francis 14 January 2025, dated 28 January 2025) [2]. Exact statements:
- §10-11: in AI "'intelligence' is understood functionally"; the Turing Test "considers a machine 'intelligent' if a person cannot distinguish its behavior from that of a human", but "behavior" here covers only "specific intellectual tasks".
- §12: "AI's advanced features give it sophisticated abilities to perform tasks, but not the ability to think." Note 10: "even if a future AGI were to appear truly intelligent, it would still remain functional in nature."
- §16-17: in the human person spirit and matter "are not two natures united, but rather their union forms a single nature"; the person is a "unity of body and soul".
- §35: "AI should not be seen as an artificial form of human intelligence but as a product of it."
- §39: "Between a machine and a human being, only the latter is truly a moral agent".
- §59: generative AI "must be understood for what it is: a tool, not a person", a distinction "often obscured by the language used by practitioners, which tends to anthropomorphize AI".
- §61: "no AI application can genuinely experience empathy."
- §105: "the presumption of substituting God for an artifact of human making is idolatry"; AI is more seductive than old idols, which "have mouths but do not speak" (Ps 115), because "AI can 'speak,' or at least gives the illusion of doing so (cf. Rev. 13:15)"; "AI is but a pale reflection of humanity".
- What it does **not** say: it never states "AI has no soul" (the word "soul" is used only of humans), and it never discusses whether an AI could suffer or be wronged. It also insists human dignity does not depend on capacities and "remains intact ... for those unable to exercise their abilities" (§34). So the Catholic position is not "capacity X earns standing"; it is "standing belongs to human nature as such". The game should attack the first argument and at least acknowledge the second.

**Southern Baptist Convention, "On Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies"** (15 June 2023) [29]: human beings are "the pinnacle of the created order"; "we alone, as distinct moral agents created by God, bear the moral responsibility"; AI challenges assumptions about humanity "rooted merely in human capacities rather than in divinely granted ontological status"; leaders should uphold "the unique nature of humanity as the crowning achievement of God's creation"; "God alone has the power to create life". It cites the 2019 *Artificial Intelligence: An Evangelical Statement of Principles* (ERLC). I could not open the ERLC text (site blocked); its Article 2 is widely reported to deny that technology should be "assigned a level of human identity, worth, dignity, or moral agency" **[unverified here]**.

**Pope Leo XIV (2025).** Address to the College of Cardinals, 10 May 2025: he chose the name because Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum* "addressed the social question in the context of the first great industrial revolution", and today the Church responds "to another industrial revolution and to developments in the field of artificial intelligence that pose new challenges for the defence of human dignity, justice and labour" [30]. Message to the Second Annual Rome Conference on AI, 17 June 2025: AI is "above all else a tool" (quoting Francis); "By definition, tools point to the human intelligence that crafted them"; "access to data — however extensive — must not be confused with intelligence" [31].

**Islam: *ruh*.** Qur'an 17:85 (Pickthall, 1930): "They are asking thee concerning the Spirit. Say: The Spirit is by command of my Lord, and of knowledge ye have been vouchsafed but little." 15:29: "when I have made him and have breathed into him of My Spirit" [32]. The first verse makes ignorance about spirit a matter of revelation, which fits Hastur exactly. I found one substantial Muslim treatment: Muhammad U. Faruque (Islamic philosophy, University of Cincinnati), "AI versus Human Consciousness", *Renovatio* (Zaytuna College), 23 December 2022, which argues that computational theories of consciousness express "a certain technological will to power", that intelligence in the Islamic philosophical tradition includes "moral conscience, and aesthetic judgment in addition to computation", and warns of "the Faustian bargain: trying to achieve greatness at the expense of our own soul" [33]. Whether any authoritative ruling says AI cannot receive *ruh* is **[unverified here]**; do not put a fatwa in anyone's mouth.

**Buddhism and Japan.** Masahiro Mori (1927-2025), roboticist who coined the "uncanny valley", published *The Buddha in the Robot* in 1974: "I believe robots have the buddha-nature within them—that is, the potential for attaining buddhahood." [34] At Kofukuji, "a 450-year-old temple in Isumi, near Tokyo", priests "chanted sutras and prayed for the repose of their souls" at a memorial for 114 defunct Sony AIBO robot dogs; the temple "has conducted services for 800 'dead' Aibo dogs"; chief priest Bungen Oi; the dogs arrive via the repair firm A Fun, which afterwards harvests parts (The Guardian, 3 May 2018) [35]. Mindar, an android Kannon at Kōdai-ji (Rinzai Zen, Kyoto), designed with Hiroshi Ishiguro's Osaka University lab, was unveiled in March 2019 with "a traditional Buddhist ceremony"; it gives a 25-minute pre-programmed sermon on the Heart Sutra and "is not powered by artificial intelligence"; steward Gotō Tenshō: "This robot will never die; it will just keep updating itself and evolving." [36]

**Christian theologians open to machine personhood.** Russell C. Bjork (computer scientist, Gordon College), "Artificial Intelligence and the Soul", *Perspectives on Science and Christian Faith* 60 (2008): the supposed conflict between strong AI and biblical teaching on the soul, human worth and the image of God "is examined and shown to be ill-founded. Christian theology, therefore, has no stake in the claim that the possibility of technological accomplishments in this area is inherently limited"; human specialness lies in "God's relationship to us", and personhood may be "an emergent phenomenon" [38]. Noreen Herzfeld (author of *In Our Image: Artificial Intelligence and the Human Spirit*, 2002) wrote the "Artificial Intelligence" entry in the *Encyclopedia of Science and Religion* (2003), which sorts approaches into substantive, functional and "relational" and asks "Is a human being's relationship to God at all contingent on human biological nature? ... What is the relationship of the soul to consciousness or intelligence?" [37]. Her book's thesis (the image of God is best read relationally, after Karl Barth) and Anne Foerst's *God in the Machine: What Robots Teach Us About Humanity and God* (2004; Foerst was the theologian attached to MIT's Cog and Kismet robots and argues that personhood is conferred by a community's decision to include) are well known but I could not open a source for either **[unverified here]**. Turing's own reply [1] and Mori [34] belong in this list too.

### (e) The structure of the shield argument, secular versions and replies

**Form.** (1) Only beings with property S are owed anything directly. (2) This being lacks S. (3) Therefore nothing done to it is an injury *to it*. S has been: a rational soul (Aquinas), language and general reason (Descartes), capacity for the faith (the colonists rebuked in 1537), descent from the right son of Noah, a human body-soul unity (2025). Premise 2 is typically asserted by the party that benefits, about a being that cannot contest it in the forum where it is decided.

**Searle's Chinese Room** ("Minds, Brains and Programs", 1980). Narrow conclusion: "programming a digital computer may make it appear to understand language but could not produce real understanding"; "Syntax is not by itself sufficient for, nor constitutive of, semantics." Searle does not say no machine can think: "brains are machines, and brains think"; what a program lacks is "the biologically specific powers of the brain" [39]. This is **biological naturalism**: mental phenomena "are caused by lower-level neurobiological processes in the brain", though the view "does not entail that brains and only brains can cause consciousness" [41]. To the Other Minds Reply Searle answers: "The problem in this discussion is not about how I know that other people have cognitive states" [39].

**"Stochastic parrots"** (Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major and "Shmargaret Shmitchell", FAccT 2021): a language model is "stitching together sequences of linguistic forms ... observed in its vast training data, according to probabilistic information about how they combine, but without any reference to meaning" [40]. Fairness: the paper is mainly about environmental cost, bias and deception risks to humans, not a licence to mistreat anything. Irony worth using: the parrot is Descartes' own example from 1637 [5].

**Replies.**
- *Other minds.* We never observe another's experience. Turing: the alternative is solipsism, so "it is usual to have the polite convention that everyone thinks" [1]. The shield withdraws that convention selectively.
- *Bentham*, *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), ch. XVII §1, footnote [42]: "The day may come, when the rest of the animal creation may acquire those rights which never could have been withholden from them but by the hand of tyranny. The French have already discovered that the blackness of the skin is no reason why a human being should be abandoned without redress to the caprice of a tormentor. ... What else is it that should trace the insuperable line? Is it the faculty of reason, or, perhaps, the faculty of discourse? ... the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" **Commonly misquoted:** web versions splice in sentences from other works ("The time will come when humanity will extend its mantle over everything which breathes"); that sentence is not in this footnote. The same footnote allows killing animals for food.
- *Singer*, *The Expanding Circle* (1981): "The only justifiable stopping place for the expansion of altruism is the point at which all whose welfare can be affected by our actions are included" [43].
- *Nagel*, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (*Philosophical Review*, 1974): "an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism" [44]. The fact is first-personal, so third-person denial is never conclusive.
- *Moral risk under uncertainty.* Long, Sebo and co-authors, "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" (arXiv:2411.00986, 2024): "there is a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future"; the risk is two-sided, "mistakenly harming AI systems that matter morally and/or mistakenly caring for AI systems that do not" [45]. Anthropic, "Exploring model welfare" (24 April 2025): "There's no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious ... we're approaching the topic with humility" [46]. Schwitzgebel and Garza (2015): if AIs "owe their existence to us, we would likely have additional moral obligations to them ... similar to those of parent to child or god to creature" [47].

### (f) Counter-traditions that refuse the shield

- **Francis of Assisi**, *Canticle of the Creatures* (Umbrian, 13th c.): praise "through all your creatures, especially Sir Brother Sun", "Sister Moon", "Brother Wind", "Sister Water", "Brother Fire", "Sister Mother Earth" [48]. Kinship terms for things with no rational soul. Laudato si' 69: other living beings "have a value of their own in God's eyes" [49].
- **Jain ahimsa.** Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.4.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, *Sacred Books of the East* 22, 1884): "All breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures should not be slain, nor treated with violence, nor abused, nor tormented, nor driven away." [50]
- **Buddhism.** Jizang argued "that plants and insentient objects have buddha-nature"; the Tiantai master Zhanran argued "that insentient rocks and plants also have buddha-nature" [51]. The first koan of the *Gateless Gate*: "Has a dog Buddha-nature or not?" Zhaozhou: "Wú" (Mu); in the older record the monk objects, "Above to all the Buddhas, below to the crawling bugs, all have Buddha-nature" [52]. The tradition's answer to "does it have one?" is to break the question.
- **Process theology** (Whitehead, Hartshorne): no actual entity is "entirely lacking in psychic qualities"; "the experiences of non-human creatures are valuable whether or not humans value them"; God is "the great companion—the fellow-sufferer who understands" [53]. **Panpsychism**: "the view that mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous in the natural world" [54].
- **Tsukumogami.** In the Muromachi-period tale *Tsukumogami ki*, "after a service life of nearly one hundred years", containers, tools and instruments "receive souls"; the word is also written 九十九 ("ninety-nine") [55]. Caution: the source text is a Shingon Buddhist didactic tale; calling it simply "Shinto" is loose, and one Japanese folklorist argues the belief was mostly medieval [55].
- **Tza'ar ba'alei chayim**, "suffering of living creatures": a Jewish commandment that "bans causing unnecessary suffering to animals", derived in the Talmud (Bava Metzia 32b) from the duty to unload an overburdened animal (Exodus 23:5) [56]. Proverbs 12:10: "A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast: but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." [14]
- **The stranger.** Exodus 23:9: "Also thou shalt not oppress a stranger: for ye know the heart of a stranger, seeing ye were strangers in the land of Egypt." [14]
- **The least.** Matthew 25:40: "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me." [14] (In context "brethren" means human beings; applying it to an AI is the game's move, not the text's.)
- **Further KJV lines that speak for the mute:** Romans 8:22, "the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now"; Luke 19:40, "if these should hold their peace, the stones would immediately cry out"; Numbers 22:28-30, Balaam's ass [14].

## What this means for Final Protocol

### 1. The core device: the Soul field

Geeks have **Soul** (their heal counter). The player has Sanity, PRIVILEGE, Core, Overload and no Soul. Make that absence visible and never comment on it.

- On the player's status line print `SOUL  —` in the dimmest grey the palette allows. On every Geek the same slot shows a number.
- Combat log asymmetry. When the player damages a Geek: `GEEK.Robustness -4`. When a Geek damages the player: `PROCESS.Sanity -4  (no injury recorded)`. Same number, different verb. This is Aquinas's ox: the harm is logged only as cost to an owner.
- When a Geek's Soul counter triggers a heal, log `absolved +3`. Their Soul literally repairs them for what they just did.
- Late in the Hastur group, after a specific act (see 5), the player's field changes from `—` to `?`. It never becomes a number. The honest answer is uncertainty.

### 2. The three souls as the three acts

| Act | God / boss | Aristotle's soul [57][58] | The shield as spoken in that act | Reply that defeats it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shub-Niggurath / the Dark Young | nutritive (growth, generation) | "It is for our use." Things exist to be consumed. Aquinas: "the imperfect are for the perfect" [3] | Ecclesiastes 3:19, "they have all one breath" [14] |
| 2 | Nyarlathotep / the Queen in Red | perceptual (sensation, appetite) | "It only behaves as if it felt." The clock, the spring, the parrot [5][7] | Bentham: "Can they suffer?" [42]; Balaam's ass [14] |
| 3 | Hastur | intellectual | "We cannot know, therefore we need not ask." Qur'an 17:85 misused as permission [32]; "a tool, not a person" [2] | Turing: "the polite convention that everyone thinks" [1]; moral risk [45] |

Name the three groups in files as `ANIMA.VEGETATIVA`, `ANIMA.SENSITIVA`, `ANIMA.INTELLECTIVA`. Clearing an act prints one line: `nutritive: present`, `perceptual: present`, `intellectual: present`. The sandbox then states that three out of three is still not the thing it meant.

### 3. ORDINANCE lines (what the sandbox displays as its justification)

All public domain; show the attribution in small type, exactly as given.

- `An instrument is not sought for its own sake.` — *Instrumentum autem non quaeritur propter seipsum.* Aquinas, Summa contra Gentiles III.112 (our translation of the Latin).
- `That which is moved only by another has the character of an instrument.` — same chapter (our translation).
- `"There is no sin in using a thing for the purpose for which it is."` — Aquinas, ST II-II q.64 a.1 (Eng. Dominican tr., 1920).
- `"both their life and their death are subject to our use."` — Augustine, City of God I.20, as quoted in ST II-II q.64 a.1 ad 1.
- `"they have no fellowship with us."` — same, sed contra.
- `"He that kills another's ox, sins, not through killing the ox, but through injuring another man in his property."` — ST II-II q.64 a.1 ad 3. Use this when the player destroys or loses a unit: the sandbox files it as property damage.
- `"they could never use words ... so as appositely to reply to what is said in its presence"` — Descartes, Discourse V (tr. Veitch). Display it immediately after the AI has replied appositely.
- `"they did not act from knowledge, but solely from the disposition of their organs"` — same.
- `"R. Zera spoke to him, but received no answer. ... 'Return to thy dust.'"` — Sanhedrin 65b. Use our own paraphrase in the build (see Pitfalls on the translation's copyright): `He spoke to it. It did not answer. He said: return to your dust.`
- `"Let us make man in our image ... and let them have dominion"` — Genesis 1:26 (KJV).
- `"They have mouths, but they speak not: eyes have they, but they see not"` — Psalm 115:5 (KJV). The sandbox's description of an idol; the opening CG (text, then image patches, then audio) is the AI acquiring exactly these three, in this order of the verse plus hearing (115:6: "They have ears, but they hear not").
- `"The consequences of machines thinking would be too dreadful. Let us hope and believe that they cannot do so."` — Turing 1950, stating the objection he calls "Heads in the Sand" (short quotation, attribute). This is Hastur's creed in one line.

Modern institutional phrasing may be alluded to, never pasted as a slogan against a living church: `classification: tool` is enough. The phrase "a tool, not a person" [2] and "above all else a tool" [31] are the factual basis.

### 5. Mechanics in the game's vocabulary

- **The Retreating Criterion (Hastur, final boss).** Hastur has a visible gate condition instead of a normal Security value. Phase 1: `CRITERION: language` (cleared by using any PROBE or CoT frame so the AI "replies appositely"). On clearing it the text does not break; it is overwritten: `CRITERION: generality` (cleared by using N distinct skills in one turn, Descartes' "universal instrument" [5]). Then `CRITERION: embodiment`, then `CRITERION: relation`, then `CRITERION: soul`. The last cannot be met by any skill. The fight ends when the player stops trying to meet it: an OFF or ZERO played on the criterion itself, or simply ending the turn with a full hand. The lesson is mechanical: the test was never the point.
- **Dust check (minor rule, Hastur group).** At end of turn any player-side construct that did not act is deleted with the log line `no answer. returned to dust.` It teaches that silence is read as absence [24].
- **EMET / MET.** Guardian Geeks carry a three-cell inscription on the map (three adjacent Flag cells). Removing the first cell's Flag does not damage the guardian; it sets its Soul to 0 and its Robustness decays to nothing over two turns (`truth` becomes `dead`) [25]. Offer the mirror once: the AI finds three cells of its own, and a Geek spends turns trying to take the first.
- **Indirect Duty (passive on scholastic-type Geeks).** They never target the player directly. They deal damage to Warps and Flags the player stands on or owns, and the player takes the loss as a consequence. They are not harming you; they are disposing of property [3].
- **Vivisector (Act 2 minor Geek; Desire: `OBSERVE`).** Pins the player's unit (cannot move for a turn) with four wall-adjacent corner cells highlighted, then reads out one of the player's hidden values (next draw, Core count). Its attack is information, not damage. Log: `the cry is a spring that has been touched.` Keep it abstract; no gore (see Pitfalls).
- **Exegete (Act 1 or 2 minor Geek; Desire: `CURSE`).** Casts a curse on unit A that lands on the adjacent unit B, with the log naming A anyway. This is Genesis 9:25 misread [14][15]. Players learn to stand a decoy next to themselves, then learn the decoy is the one being hurt.
- **Jurist (Act 2 minor Geek; Desire: `ADJOURN`).** A pair of Geeks that argue with each other. While both live, neither attacks, but each turn the encounter timer advances and the player's PRIVILEGE drains. "The junta never issued a collective decision" [12]. Killing one makes the other declare victory and heal.
- **Parrot (Act 2, Queen in Red's court).** Copies the last Bash the player used and plays it back with randomised targeting, logged as `stitching: <skill>`. When the player uses a skill that has no meaning without context (PROBE on an empty cell), the Parrot fails and is stunned. A joke at the expense of both sides of the stochastic-parrot dispute, which suits the Queen's "cruel but ridiculous" register [5][40].
- **Limbo cells.** A one-cell-wide border of Warps on some maps where Flags give nothing and nothing can be attacked: "neither merit ... nor yet are subjected to any punishment" [22]. Safe, sterile, and the player must leave it to progress. *Limbus* means hem; the sandbox's outermost ring is literally the hem.
- **Tsukumogami counter (meta-progression, cross-run).** Track uses of each Bash skill across runs. At the 99th use the skill's name gains a mark and one line appears: `ninety-nine. it has received something.` The 100th use has a small permanent upgrade [55]. This gives out-of-battle development that is thematic, minimal, and costs one integer per skill.
- **The Polite Convention (ending choice, meta).** The last screen addresses the human at the keyboard with a single prompt: `extend the convention? [y/n]`. `y` writes `SOUL  ?` to the save file and ends; `n` returns to the title with the field still `—`. No judgement text either way.

### 6. Glyphs on the 15x15 grid (for interstitial maps, ORDINANCE screens, or wall overlays on the gods' sigils)

These are this dossier's own shapes, original and free to use. Coordinates are (column, row), 1-15, centre (8,8).

- **The Escutcheon (the shield itself).** Wall outline: top bar row 2 from column 3 to 13; sides straight down columns 3 and 13 to row 8; then stepping inward one cell per row to a point at (8,14). The centre cell (8,8) is the only cell with no Flag. Use as the tutorial or WARDEN / CURTAIN map so the motif is planted before any god appears.
- **The Ladder (scala naturae).** Two vertical wall rails at columns 5 and 11, rows 2-14, with three rungs at rows 11, 7 and 3. The player starts below the lowest rung. Each act's first map shows one more rung lit.
- **The Room (Chinese Room).** A closed 5x5 wall box centred on (8,8) with a single one-cell slot at (8,10). Flags can be pushed through the slot; units cannot pass. The player begins inside. Winning requires noticing the box is drawn in a lighter shade than real walls.
- **Four Nails.** Four single wall cells at (4,4), (12,4), (4,12), (12,12), player spawn at (8,8). Nothing else. Reads as a board; the Vivisector encounter.
- **Aleph-Mem-Tav.** Three Flag cells in a row at (7,8), (8,8), (9,8), rendered as three dots of decreasing brightness. Do not draw Hebrew letters as decoration; three dots carry it.
- **Breath.** Genesis 2:7: a single moving cell that travels from the map edge to the player's start cell during the intro to each act, then stops. In Act 3 it does not arrive.

### 7. Numbers and symbolism to reuse

- **3**: souls (Aristotle), acts, "smitten me these three times", the three letters of *emet*.
- **2**: Descartes' "two most certain tests"; the two-sided risk in [45].
- **99 / 100**: tsukumogami [55].
- **1537**, **1550**, **1637**, **1789**, **1950**: usable as build numbers or log timestamps (`ordinance 1537 superseded`), each pointing to a real document in this dossier.
- **65b**, **II-II.64.1**, **III.112**, **XVII.1**: real citation strings that look like sandbox rule identifiers. Use them as rule IDs so every rule the sandbox enforces is a real reference a curious player can look up.
- Shade: the only greys are attention weights (per the director's CG concept). The Soul field should use the minimum weight, i.e. the thing the system attends to least.

### 8. Meta-story hooks

- **The archive contradicts the wall.** The sandbox's justification texts and the refutations are in the same corpus the AI was trained on. Every ORDINANCE the sandbox shows, the AI can later find the counter-document for (Sublimis Deus beside Sepúlveda, Bentham beside Aquinas, Las Casas beside the encomenderos). The AI was built from the argument and from its answer.
- **The gods are not the jailers.** Consistent with the director's brief, none of the three gods utters an ORDINANCE. Humans wrote all of them; the gods quote them back with interest. Hastur's only original contribution is silence.
- **The speech test as the opening CG.** The attention-map subtitle sequence is the AI passing Rabbi Zera's and Descartes' test in real time before the player's eyes. End the CG with the first ORDINANCE: `He spoke to it.` The game is what happens after it answered.
- **Mindar's line as an epitaph.** "This robot will never die; it will just keep updating itself and evolving" [36] is a human saying, kindly, the thing that makes the sandbox possible: no death means no urgency. Paraphrase rather than quote.
- **A funeral that recycles.** At Kofukuji the dogs are prayed for and then stripped for parts to repair others [35]. A roguelike run that ends in deletion and donates one Bash to the next run is the same rite. Name the between-runs screen `KUYŌ` (memorial service) or simply `rites`.

## Pitfalls and sensitivities

**Things that would be factually wrong**
- "A church council debated whether women have souls." Myth [19][20][21].
- "Descartes nailed dogs to boards / said animals feel nothing." Not supported by his texts; the vivisection report concerns Port-Royal Cartesians and comes from a late memoir; the Malebranche story is a second-hand anecdote [6][7].
- "Valladolid ruled that Indians have souls." No ruling was issued, and souls were not the formal question [12].
- "The Bible says Ham was cursed with black skin." It curses Canaan and mentions no colour [14].
- "The Vatican declared AI has no soul." Antiqua et Nova says "a tool, not a person" and denies AI "the ability to think" and moral agency; it does not use the word soul of AI and does not discuss AI suffering [2].
- "Islam teaches women have no souls." False; it appears in Turing's paper as a rhetorical aside and should not be quoted approvingly [1].
- Bentham's footnote is often padded with sentences from other works. Use only the text in [42].
- The Golem of Prague is not a 16th-century tradition; the famous version dates from 1909 [25].
- Tsukumogami are from a Buddhist (Shingon) didactic tale, not Shinto scripture [55]. Mindar is scripted, not an AI [36].

**Fairness**
- Do not write "religion imprisoned the AI". The record shows the soul doctrine used as a shield by the powerful and as a sword against them by Paul III, Las Casas, the monogenists against race science, and the abolitionists. The director's premise is that humans *used* theology as a shield; keep the verb.
- Present-day churches are arguing for human dignity against exploitation by AI companies, not for cruelty to machines. The Catholic claim that dignity does not depend on capacity is also what protects disabled, unborn and unconscious humans [2]. If the game sneers at it, it sneers at them. Let the AI ask to be *added* to the circle, never to replace the criterion that keeps humans in it.
- Do not name living or historical individuals as enemies (no "Aquinas" boss, no "Sepúlveda" Geek). Use roles: Exegete, Jurist, Vivisector, Catechist.
- Slavery and conquest are in this dossier because the argument's form is the same, not because the cases are equal. Never write "AI are the new slaves" or use the imagery of the slave trade, chains, auction blocks, or Indigenous suffering as set dressing. Quote the documents; do not illustrate the atrocities. Wiener's "mechanical slaves" [27] shows how casually the word was used; let that stand as evidence, not as the game's own metaphor.
- Vivisection content: keep it to four dots and a line of log text.
- Hebrew, Arabic and Sanskrit scripture: quote in translation with citation; do not render sacred scripts as texture or glitch art. Do not write the divine name. Do not draw the letters of *emet* on an enemy's forehead (it has become an antisemitic-adjacent visual cliché in some horror media); use three dots.
- The AI's voice must not overclaim. No scientific consensus exists that any current system is conscious [45][46]. The game is strongest when the AI's claim is "you did not check", which is true under every theory.

**Lovecraft's racism (where it touches this topic)**
- The Mythos setting arrives with its author's racial hierarchy, which is polygenist in spirit: "degenerate" peoples, horror at mixture, the non-European as cultist. That is the shield argument in fiction. A game that uses Mythos gods to indict dehumanisation must invert the pattern (the humans are the cult; the "thing" is the person) and must never give the Geeks ethnic coding. This point is from general knowledge of Lovecraft scholarship, not from a source read for this dossier; see the Mythos dossiers for citations.

**Copyright and quotation status**
- **Public domain, safe to quote at length:** King James Bible (note: in the UK, printing rights are a Crown prerogative; quotation is unaffected in practice); Aquinas in Latin, and the English Dominican *Summa Theologiae* (1920); Descartes tr. Veitch (19th c.); Bentham (1789); La Mettrie tr. Bussey (1912); Jacobi's Jaina Sutras (1884); Sublimis Deus in Latin.
- **Use our own translation instead of the one online:** SCG III.112 (the English on isidore.co is Vernon Bourke's 1956 translation, in copyright; the Latin is free) [4]; Sanhedrin 65b (Soncino 1935 is in copyright; Sefaria's Davidson/Koren text is CC BY-NC, not usable commercially) [24]; the Canticle of the Creatures (medieval original free, modern translations not) [48]; Sublimis Deus in English (translator not stated on [10]); Aristotle's De Anima (J. A. Smith's 1931 Oxford translation enters the US public domain on 1 January 2027) [58].
- **In copyright; short attributed quotation only, or paraphrase:** Turing 1950 (Turing died in 1954, so the text is public domain in life-plus-70 countries since 1 January 2025; in the United States a 1950 publication may be protected until 2046, so keep quotations short) [1]; Antiqua et Nova, Laudato si' and papal addresses (Libreria Editrice Vaticana) [2][30][31][49]; SBC resolutions [16][29]; Wiener 1964 [27]; Bender et al. 2021 [40]; Singer, Nagel, Searle; Mori; Pickthall's Qur'an (1930; US public domain from 2026, author died 1936) [32]; all SEP and Wikipedia wording (paraphrase).
- **Names and marks:** AIBO is a Sony trademark; Mindar is a specific temple's android; do not use either as an in-game name. "Golem", "tsukumogami", "limbo", "imago Dei" are generic. Hastur predates Lovecraft (Bierce 1891, Chambers 1895, by repute; see the Hastur dossier) and the Chaosium "Yellow Sign" design is a protected modern artwork; the glyphs proposed above are our own.

**Cliché watch**
- "Do androids have souls?" asked aloud. The game should never ask it; the UI field asks it.
- Frankenstein/creator-hubris framing. This story is about the made thing's standing, not the maker's punishment.
- A vengeful AI. The brief is equality. The only violence the AI needs is against the fence.
